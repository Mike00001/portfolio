Understanding the Deauth attack (802.11w)

Technical analysis of Wi-Fi deauthentication frames and how the 802.11w standard mitigates these vulnerabilities.

aireplay-ng

root@kali:~# aireplay-ng --deauth 10 -a 00:11:22:33:44:55 -c AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF wlan1mon

10:42:01 Waiting for beacon frame (BSSID: 00:11:22:33:44:55) on channel 6

10:42:02 Sending 64 directed DeAuth. STMAC: [AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF] [ 5|62 ACKs]

10:42:03 Sending 64 directed DeAuth. STMAC: [AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF] [ 8|53 ACKs]

[+] Handshake captured! WPA handshake: 00:11:22:33:44:55

Vulnerability Mechanism

Deauthentication frames were designed in the original 802.11 standard (1997) to allow a client or access point (AP) to gracefully terminate a connection. The major issue is that these management frames are historically neither encrypted nor authenticated.

An attacker therefore does not need to know the network's WPA/WPA2 security key to send these frames. They simply need to forge a packet (MAC Spoofing) on behalf of the Router or Client, and the connection is broken.

What is it used for?

  • Denial of Service (DoS): Preventing a specific client (or everyone) from connecting.
  • WPA Handshake Capture: The main goal of the attack is to force the victim to reconnect automatically. During this reconnection, the attacker listens to the EAPOL key exchange (4-Way Handshake) to subsequently crack it offline (bruteforce).
  • Rogue AP / Captive Portal (Evil Twin): Disconnecting the target from the real access point to force them to connect to yours (which has the same name).

The Solution: 802.11w (PMF)

In 2009, the 802.11w amendment introduced Protected Management Frames (PMF). It adds a cryptographic mechanism (MIC - Message Integrity Code) to disconnection frames. Today, it is mandatory for WPA3 certification, permanently neutralizing this attack vector.
